Assignment 1

Comparing Rivers, Identity, and History in Two Poems

Introduction to this artifact

Assignment focus: A comparative literary analysis of Richard Blanco’s “Complaint of the Río Grande” and Langston Hughes’s “The Negro Speaks of Rivers.”

Why I selected it: This essay is the clearest example of my central learning claim. It shows how close reading, interpretation, and textual evidence can reveal different meanings in a shared symbol. By comparing the rivers in both poems, I examine how history, identity, community, and belonging are represented through imagery, repetition, and symbolism.

What I learned: I practiced moving beyond a poem’s literal subject to explain how a writer’s choices shape meaning. I also learned that two texts can use a similar image while communicating contrasting perspectives.

Complete assignment

The poems "Complaint of El rio grande" by Richard Blanco and "The Negro Speaks of Rivers" by Langston Hughes both make use of rivers as central metaphors in order to examine cultural identities, communities, and history. Even though both poets deal with these themes, they do so from different perspectives. Hughes depicts the rivers in the United States, Africa, and the Middle East as flowing symbols of Black ancestry and of communal resilience, whereas Blanco employs the river which forms the border between the U.S. and Mexico in order to highlight his complaint about the separation between people. Using imagery, repetition, and symbolism, both poems illustrate how identity is influenced by historical experience. However, Hughes stresses cultural stability and Blanco concentrates on separation and borders.

In "The Negro Speaks of Rivers" Hughes associates the speaker's identity with the long-standing history of African and African American civilization. The line "I've known rivers" (line 1) gives the speaker a voice which seems to be older than that of any individual person. The fact that Hughes refers to the Euphrates, the Congo, the Nile and the Mississippi shows a progression from ancient Africa and Mesopotamia to the United States; these rivers link important events in human history such as the development of early civilizations, the building of the pyramids, and the fight against slavery. The phrase "My soul has grown deep like the rivers" (line 10) uses the river as a metaphor for the depth of Black history and experience. Although oppression is part of that history, the poem's calm and dignified tone emphasizes survival, wisdom and endurance.

The river that Blanco refers to is a symbol of a border. In his poem "Complaints of the Rio Grande" the river represents the division between "wetbacks and gringos" (line 24). Unlike Hughes's rivers, which act as symbols of emotion and of history carried throughout the generations, Blanco's river shows a world in which things are divided rather than coming together which upsets him because the river “Was meant for all things to meet” (line 1); he employs imagery in order to express his anger and his conservations with the river. Hughes, on the other hand, is nostalgic and almost joyful, using the water in the way it was intended to connect different generations of history, whereas Blanco is angry because his river is having the opposite effect.

The poets also vary in the communities they refer to. Hughes uses a wide, general voice that stands for many generations of Black people; the repeated phrase “I've known rivers” (lines 1,2,9) gives the poem a ceremonial touch and makes it seem as if the speaker holds the memories of whole civilizations. Blanco's voice, on the other hand, is more severe and expresses the idea that his river was meant to join the communities together but instead became a barrier. While Hughes's speaker travels across continents and throughout thousands of years, Blanco's speaker looks back in a more limited but emotionally intricate way through the perspective of personal memory. Hughes's speaker is recalling the past, whereas Blanco's speaker is making a direct complaint.

Even though there is this difference, both poets describe water as a powerful force. Rivers always flow and carry traces of different communities to one another, but they can also divide those same communities if people attempt to do so. Hughes makes use of this characteristic to illustrate cultural continuity, since the speaker is able to see himself in both ancient civilizations and in American history. The image of the Mississippi turning "all golden in the sunset" (line 7) changes a river linked with slavery into one that symbolizes beauty and hope, especially because of its connection with Abraham Lincoln's journey to New Orleans. Blanco employs the same quality to support his argument by saying that the river was meant to "turn eons of loveless rocks into lovesick pebbles and carry them as humble gifts back to the sea which brings life back to me" (lines 5-7). However, the countries that we have created have fitted these borders together and drawn lines stating "you are here and not there" (lines 16-17).

The structure of the poems highlights these opposing ideas. Hughes makes use of repetition, short lines, and a list of rivers which have geographical importance. By referring repeatedly to knowing the rivers he gives a sense of stability, which makes history seem continuous and firmly established. In contrast, Blanco deals with the river in a more abandoned and cruel way. Hughes's language comes across as confident and universal, whereas that of Blanco suggests a feeling of loss and remoteness. As a result, Hughes's river strengthens the speaker's sense of belonging, while Blanco's river shows the tension between belonging and separation.

Langston Hughes and Richard Blanco both make use of rivers as symbols of identity, history, and a sense of belonging. In Hughes’s poem “The Negro Speaks of Rivers” the rivers stand for the deep origins and enduring history of Black civilization and link the speaker’s identity with various generations of people and places in history. In Blanco’s poem “The Complaint of the Río Grande” the river illustrates the experiences of people who have been separated by borders, migration, and cultural change. Although Hughes depicts the river as a source of strength and continuity that preserves the collective history, Blanco shows the Río Grande as a witness to separation and to the difficulties faced by those who cross or who live beside the river. The two poems together demonstrate that rivers can connect people to their heritage while at the same time showing the changes, divisions, and uncertainty that affect their identities.
